where does a $200 agency hour go?

In our normalized model, operating costs consume $153. Roughly $93 pays delivery payroll and $60 funds the agency around it. The remaining $47 is margin room.

Bigger is not worse. But it is a different machine—and clients should know which parts of that machine they actually need.

What the audit actually found

agencies show the work.
they rarely show the machine.

0/10

publish a current primary-source commercial rate card.

0/10

publish an audited Toronto role mix or delivery ratio.

0/10

disclose enough lease detail to calculate exact office overhead.

That means a precise agency-by-agency cost ranking would be theatre. The useful comparison is simpler: how large is the operating system, and how much of it does your problem need?

Client useThree questions worth asking

before you hire any agency, ask this.

  1. 01

    Who will actually work on my business?

    Ask for the named delivery team—not only the senior people in the pitch.

  2. 02

    How many paid hours make the work?

    Separate strategy, creative and design time from coordination, reporting and layers of review.

  3. 03

    What changes if the team changes?

    A clear commercial model should explain what happens to the fee when scope, seniority or staffing moves.

Model and source notes ＋

The $200 hour

The default scenario uses a round $200 planning rate—not a claimed market average—plus $132,000 in annual loaded people cost per employee, a 73% delivery share, 1,425 collected hours per delivery employee, 100 square feet per employee, $68 gross occupancy per square foot and other operating cost equal to 15% of people cost. The modelled cost floor is $153 per delivery hour.

The Toronto ranges

Ranges use public company bands, visible associated-profile counts, office structure and network allocation. They are intentionally broad. No agency supplied or verified the model.

The point

buy the machine the problem needs.

Large infrastructure is valuable when the assignment needs it. When it doesn't, a smaller team can put more of the conversation—and the budget—closer to the people making the work.
