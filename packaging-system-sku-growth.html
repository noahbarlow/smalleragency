Thinking / Packaging systems

Your fifteenth SKU should not break the brand.

A good range grows. A weak system just accumulates packaging.

By Noah Barlow / Updated October 21, 2026 / 8 minute read

Smaller / Field note
One family.More shelves.

SKU architecture / Range design / Packaging

Written from the work, not from a content calendar.

The first pack gets the presentation. The fifteenth pack reveals whether there was ever a system.

A range has two jobs at once.

Every product needs to look related to the brand and different from the product beside it. Too much consistency makes navigation difficult. Too much variation turns the shelf into a family argument.

The useful tension

Recognition tells people it is yours. Navigation tells them which one is for them.

Start with architecture, not colour.

Before assigning flavour colours, decide how the range is organized. Product line, format, benefit, strength, audience, flavour and pack size may all compete for attention.

Write the hierarchy before styling it. If the team cannot agree on what comes first in words, a colour palette will not fix it.

Design the ugly edge cases early.

The longest name

If the layout only works for three letters, it does not work.

The smallest pack

A system designed on a carton can collapse on a sachet or bottle neck.

The densest claim set

Benefits and mandatory language need a rule, not a negotiation on every SKU.

The retailer thumbnail

At small scale, pack shape, brand block and product cue have to do more than photography.

The future line

Leave enough range for an adjacent product family without making today’s pack vague.

Build rules people can actually use.

A packaging toolkit should answer everyday questions without turning every answer into a committee meeting. What is fixed? What can flex? How is a new flavour introduced? Where do claims go? What changes by format?

Useful guidelines show examples, exceptions and real files. A mood board with a logo on it is not a production system.

Know when the range needs repair.

  • 01

    The brand block moves on every SKU.

  • 02

    Colour is the only navigation device.

  • 03

    Every launch starts with a blank artboard.

  • 04

    Retailers or customers confuse products in the range.

  • 05

    The newest SKU looks more current than the rest of the brand.

Those are not isolated layout problems. They are signs the system needs a decision.

Questions that come up.

What is a packaging system?

A packaging system is the set of decisions that keeps a range recognizable while helping shoppers distinguish products, flavours, formats and benefits.

When should a brand build a packaging system?

Before the range becomes hard to manage. If every new SKU needs a new debate about colour, claims, hierarchy or naming, the system is already late.

Does every SKU need its own colour?

No. Colour can help navigation, but it should not carry the entire job. Naming, typography, imagery, numbers and format cues can work together.

What belongs in a packaging toolkit?

Core layouts, hierarchy rules, SKU logic, colour and image systems, copy zones, regulatory guidance, production specifications and examples of edge cases.
