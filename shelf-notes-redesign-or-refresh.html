Does your packaging need a redesign or a refresh?

A practical way to decide whether the current packaging needs sharper expression or a new system built around a changed business.

By Noah BarlowSeptember 9, 20266 minute read
03
Brand decisions
6 minute read
Toronto

Refresh the packaging when the brand's promise, product and shelf logic are still right but the expression has gone stale. Redesign it when the business, audience, category or product range has changed enough that the old system is now solving the wrong problem.

Start with what changed.

“It looks dated” may be true. It is rarely the whole brief.

Maybe the product moved from specialty retail to grocery. Maybe three SKUs became thirty. Maybe the premium cues are now category wallpaper. Maybe shoppers still cannot tell the flavours apart. Or maybe a new marketing lead is simply tired of looking at the same label.

Those are not the same problem. They should not produce the same project.

Do not spend rebrand money to solve boredom. Do not use a refresh to avoid a real business change.

A refresh makes sense when:

01

The positioning is still useful and customers understand what the brand stands for.

02

The logo and strongest distinctive assets still carry recognition worth protecting.

03

The range architecture works, but type, colour, imagery or hierarchy need discipline.

04

The product has not fundamentally changed; the package simply undersells it.

A redesign is probably overdue when:

01

The business is entering a new category, channel or price tier the old brand cannot credibly hold.

02

Customers misunderstand the product or choose the wrong SKU.

03

Every extension needs exceptions, patches and another tiny rule.

04

The packaging tells an old strategy while the company sells a new one.

05

The only recognizable asset is the logo, and even that is doing very little work.

Protect what people already know.

A redesign does not earn points for changing everything.

Sometimes the most valuable move is keeping the colour people find from the other end of the aisle. Or the shape they recognize in a fridge. Or the part of the name customers actually use.

The job is to separate useful equity from accumulated habit. Keep the first. Question the second.

Five questions before deciding

  1. Has the business strategy changed, or are we just tired of the design?
  2. What do customers recognize today that we would be foolish to throw away?
  3. Where does the current pack fail: attention, understanding, navigation, credibility or production?
  4. Can those failures be fixed inside the current identity?
  5. Will the answer still work when the next five SKUs arrive?

If the answers point to expression, refresh it. If they point to meaning, architecture or a new buying context, redesign it properly.

The cheapest project is the one sized to the real problem.

A refresh should not quietly become a rebrand halfway through. A redesign should not be stripped down until it is only new typography on the same broken system.

Figure out which decision you are making before anyone opens Illustrator. It saves time, money and several rounds of increasingly strange comps.
