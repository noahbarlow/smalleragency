Bilingual packaging without the last-minute squeeze.

English and French are not a production problem. They are a design input.

By Noah Barlow / Updated October 21, 2026 / 9 minute read

Smaller / Field note
Two languages.One hierarchy.

Bilingual packaging / Food + beverage / Canada

Written from the work, not from a content calendar.

In Canada, most mandatory information on consumer prepackaged food must appear in English and French. The strongest packs make room for both before the layout starts pretending space is unlimited.

What is generally required?

For consumer prepackaged food, the Canadian Food Inspection Agency says mandatory information generally has to appear in both official languages. That includes core items such as the common name. Product-specific rules, exemptions and provincial requirements can also apply.

This is not legal advice, and the label owner remains responsible for compliance. Use the CFIA Industry Labelling Tool and qualified regulatory support for the actual product.

Design both languages at the same time.

Translation changes more than character count. French can be longer. A product descriptor that sits neatly in English may need a different line break, scale or relationship to the flavour.

The design rule

Do not approve an English front panel and “add French later.” There is no later. There is only a second hierarchy fighting for the same space.

Build with real bilingual copy early. Test the longest product name, the smallest format and the least forgiving SKU. That is where the system tells the truth.

One panel or two?

CFIA guidance allows several approaches. English and French information may share one principal display panel. In some circumstances, separate English and French principal display panels can be used when surfaces have the required size and prominence.

The right answer depends on the pack structure, retail orientation and what the product needs to communicate first. A pouch, can and carton do not offer the same real estate.

Build a hierarchy that can breathe.

Lead once

Decide what the shopper needs first: brand, product, flavour or benefit. Bilingual does not mean every line has equal visual weight.

Pair deliberately

Keep equivalent English and French information visibly connected. Do not make the shopper hunt for the other half.

Protect panels

Nutrition Facts, ingredients, allergens, net quantity and other prescribed information need real, measured space.

Test the range

A layout that only works for “Lime / Lime” is not a system. Use the longest names and densest back panel during design.

The back panel is still part of the brand.

Regulated information does not need to look abandoned. Spacing, grid, type choices, section order and contrast can make a dense panel feel considered without tampering with prescribed formats.

The goal is not to decorate the requirements. It is to integrate them so the package still feels like one object.

Use current official guidance.

Rules change and product categories differ. Start with the current CFIA checklist and category-specific tools, then confirm the copy with the people responsible for compliance.

Questions that come up.

Does Canadian food packaging need English and French?

In general, mandatory information on consumer prepackaged food must appear in both English and French, subject to specific exemptions and product rules.

Can English and French use different type sizes?

Applicable minimum type-height rules still apply. CFIA notes that the languages are not always required to use equal type heights, although equal presentation can improve legibility and balance.

Should translation happen after the design is approved?

No. French often changes line length and hierarchy. Plan both languages while the system is being designed.

Who is responsible for regulatory compliance?

The manufacturer or distributor is responsible for the label. Designers should work from approved copy and coordinate with qualified regulatory and legal advisers where needed.
